Introduction and Motivation

Liver cirrhosis is a chronic and progressive disease in which healthy liver tissue is replaced by scar tissue, leading to impaired liver function and potentially severe complications. Accurate staging is important for treatment planning, disease monitoring, and long-term patient care. However, distinguishing between disease stages can be difficult because many clinical indicators overlap throughout disease progression.

Traditional diagnosis often depends on combinations of laboratory testing, medical imaging interpretation, and physician judgment. As a result, liver disease assessment can be time-consuming and may vary depending on the available clinical information and the physician’s experience.

This project explores the use of machine learning techniques for liver cirrhosis stage classification using both structured clinical datasets and ultrasound imaging data. Decision Trees, Random Forests, Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural networks, and preliminary Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) models were investigated to compare model accuracy, interpretability, and image-based classification potential.

Machine learning provides an opportunity to analyze multiple clinical variables simultaneously and identify complex nonlinear relationships within patient data that may not be obvious through traditional analysis methods. The goal of this work was to evaluate how effectively different machine learning models could predict liver cirrhosis stage while exploring how structured clinical records and imaging-based workflows may support more integrated liver disease assessment systems.

Project Scope

This project explored liver cirrhosis stage classification using both structured clinical datasets and liver ultrasound imaging data. Multiple machine learning workflows were implemented and evaluated in MATLAB in order to compare classification accuracy, interpretability, feature importance behavior, and the ability of different architectures to capture nonlinear relationships within clinical and image-based datasets.

Tabular Clinical Data Models

Decision Tree, Random Forest, and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) models were trained using structured clinical datasets containing laboratory measurements, demographic information, and physician-observed clinical indicators associated with liver disease progression.

The tabular workflow focused on supervised multiclass classification of liver cirrhosis stages 1–3 while also evaluating model interpretability, feature importance, and classification consistency across different machine learning architectures.

Medical Imaging Models

Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) approaches were explored using grayscale liver ultrasound image datasets in order to investigate imaging-based liver fibrosis classification directly from medical scans.

The CNN workflow included image resizing, zero-padding, dataset balancing, and architecture development using convolutional layers, pooling layers, batch normalization, and softmax classification layers implemented in MATLAB.

Integrated Clinical Perspective

Although implemented separately, the structured clinical workflow and imaging workflow represent closely connected components of the same clinical assessment process.

Many variables within the structured dataset, including ascites, hepatomegaly, edema, and spiders, are often derived from physician interpretation of imaging studies or physical examination findings before being entered numerically into patient records.

As a result, combining structured clinical data with direct imaging analysis may provide a more comprehensive and clinically realistic approach to liver cirrhosis assessment.

Clinical Records Dataset

The machine learning models developed in this project were trained using a publicly available liver cirrhosis dataset derived from Mayo Clinic patient records. The dataset was obtained from the Kaggle repository Liver Cirrhosis Stage Classification and contained structured demographic information, laboratory measurements, and physician-observed clinical indicators associated with liver disease progression.

Cirrhosis stages 1–3 were used as the multiclass classification targets for supervised machine learning.

Clinical Data Preprocessing

Prior to model training, the dataset was preprocessed in MATLAB to improve data quality and ensure compatibility with machine learning algorithms. The preprocessing pipeline described below was consistently applied across all tabular machine learning models used in this project, including the Decision Tree, Random Forest, and MLP neural network models.

  • Removed rows containing missing target classification labels
  • Retained only cirrhosis stages 1–3 for classification
  • Removed non-predictive variables including Stage, ID, N_Days, Drug, and Status
  • Converted categorical variables into numerical format using one-hot encoding
  • Handled missing numerical values through interpolation and mean-value replacement
  • Assigned missing categorical values to a separate category before encoding
  • Partitioned the dataset into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing subsets
  • Applied z-score normalization using training set statistics to prevent data leakage

Many variables within the structured dataset, including ascites, hepatomegaly, edema, and spiders, are often derived from physician interpretation of imaging studies or physical examination findings before being entered numerically into patient records.

Ultrasound Imaging Dataset

In addition to the structured clinical dataset, an image-based dataset was explored for liver fibrosis stage classification using ultrasound imaging data. The dataset consisted of approximately 6,323 grayscale ultrasound images organized into five classification categories, f0 through f4, representing increasing stages of liver fibrosis progression from no fibrosis to severe fibrosis.

The dataset was derived from the work of Y. Joo et al., titled “Classification of Liver Fibrosis From Heterogeneous Ultrasound Image”, published in IEEE Access in 2023. The ultrasound images were collected from Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital and used for CNN training and validation purposes.

CNN Image Preprocessing

Prior to CNN training, the ultrasound images were preprocessed in MATLAB to create a standardized dataset suitable for convolutional neural network classification. The CNN imaging pipeline used separate image-specific preprocessing procedures prior to architecture development and model training.

  • Obtained the dimensions of the first image to define a target image size
  • Resized all grayscale ultrasound images to 480 × 640 × 1
  • Preserved original images by saving resized copies into a separate dataset directory
  • Maintained image aspect ratios during resizing
  • Applied center zero-padding to preserve target dimensions
  • Iterated through fibrosis stage subfolders, f0 through f4, during processing
  • Created balanced training subsets using 400 images from each fibrosis stage category
  • Organized resized images into stage-specific class folders for CNN training

The balanced dataset approach was used to reduce training bias caused by unequal numbers of images between fibrosis stage categories. The resulting standardized dataset was then used for CNN architecture development and training.

Machine Learning Methods

Multiple machine learning approaches were explored throughout this project in order to compare model interpretability, ensemble learning performance, nonlinear classification capability, and image-based medical analysis for liver cirrhosis staging.

Decision Tree

The Decision Tree model was selected as the baseline classifier because it was the simplest and most interpretable machine learning approach explored in this project. Tree depth was limited during training to reduce overfitting while maintaining interpretability of the classification process. The model was trained using the preprocessed clinical dataset and evaluated using the same 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing partitioning strategy applied throughout the project.

The model provided a transparent framework for evaluating how individual clinical features contributed to liver cirrhosis stage classification and served as a comparison point for the more advanced ensemble and neural network models explored later in the project.

Generalized Decision Tree classification structure used as the baseline interpretable machine learning model.

Random Forest

Built-In MATLAB Implementation

The Random Forest model was implemented using MATLAB’s built-in fitcensemble function with bootstrap aggregation (bagging). Multiple decision trees were trained using randomized subsets of both the training data and feature space in order to improve generalization performance and reduce correlation between trees.

Predictions from individual trees were combined using majority voting to produce the final classification output, while feature importance analysis was used to identify the most influential clinical variables associated with liver cirrhosis staging.

From-Scratch Implementation

A second Random Forest model was developed from scratch in order to better understand the internal mechanics of ensemble learning algorithms. The custom implementation utilized bootstrap sampling, recursive decision tree construction, randomized feature selection, and majority voting aggregation across independently trained trees.

Although computationally slower than MATLAB’s built-in implementation, the from-scratch model provided greater insight into how Random Forest classifiers operate internally and how ensemble learning improves classification performance.

Generalized Random Forest ensemble architecture utilizing multiple independently trained Decision Trees.

MLP Neural Network

A custom multilayer perceptron (MLP) neural network was manually implemented in MATLAB for multiclass liver cirrhosis stage classification. Rather than relying on MATLAB’s built-in neural network training tools, the network was developed manually in order to provide direct control over network architecture, forward propagation, and backpropagation implementation.

The final network architecture consisted of an input layer, two hidden layers containing 64 and 32 neurons respectively, and an output layer containing three neurons corresponding to cirrhosis stages 1–3. ReLU activation functions were applied to the hidden layers, while the output layer utilized softmax activation for multiclass probability prediction.

The network was trained using gradient descent optimization with cross-entropy loss. During training, forward propagation was used to compute class probabilities, while backpropagation was manually implemented using the chain rule to calculate gradients and iteratively update network parameters.

Generalized Multilayer Perceptron neural network architecture used for nonlinear multiclass liver cirrhosis classification.

CNN Model

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) were explored for direct ultrasound image-based liver fibrosis classification. Unlike the tabular machine learning approaches used earlier in the project, CNNs operate directly on imaging data and are capable of automatically extracting hierarchical spatial features using convolutional filters and pooling operations.

The CNN architecture was developed in MATLAB and accepted grayscale ultrasound images with dimensions of 480 × 640 × 1. The network consisted of four convolutional blocks utilizing convolutional layers, batch normalization, ReLU activation functions, and max pooling operations for progressive feature extraction.

The number of learned filters increased throughout the network from 8 to 16, 32, and 64 filters in order to capture increasingly complex imaging features associated with liver fibrosis progression.

The final classification stage utilized a fully connected layer, softmax layer, and classification layer to assign ultrasound images into one of five fibrosis categories, f0 through f4.

The network was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, a mini-batch size of 32, and a maximum of 20 training epochs. Validation monitoring and early stopping criteria were also implemented during training to reduce overfitting and preserve the best-performing validation network.

Decision Tree Results

The Decision Tree model achieved classification accuracies ranging from approximately 70–74% when predicting liver cirrhosis stage. Analysis of the confusion matrix showed that Stage 2 was the most difficult classification category because many clinical indicators overlap with neighboring disease stages. In contrast, the model demonstrated stronger performance when identifying Stage 1 and Stage 3 cases, where clinical distinctions were more pronounced. Overall, the Decision Tree provided a simple and interpretable baseline for comparison against the more advanced ensemble and neural network models explored later in the project.

Decision Tree Feature Importance

Feature importance analysis showed that several laboratory-based clinical variables contributed strongly to cirrhosis stage classification. Random Forest Results

The Random Forest model demonstrated strong overall performance for liver cirrhosis stage classification. The built-in MATLAB Random Forest achieved a highest testing accuracy of 88.77%, while the from-scratch implementation achieved 81.81%. The lower performance of the from-scratch version was expected because the custom implementation was primarily developed to better understand the internal behavior of ensemble learning algorithms rather than maximize optimization efficiency.

Baseline Random Forest Confusion Matrix

Random Forest Model Comparison

The baseline confusion matrix showed strong classification performance across all three cirrhosis stages, with most predictions concentrated along the diagonal. Misclassifications occurred primarily between neighboring stages, particularly between Stages 1 and 2 and between Stages 2 and 3. This behavior is reasonable because many clinical indicators overlap between adjacent stages of liver disease progression.

Feature removal experiments demonstrated that laboratory measurements were the most important predictors within the dataset. When laboratory-based features were removed, testing accuracy decreased significantly from 88.77% to 66.19%. In contrast, removing symptom-related features only reduced accuracy to 85.76%, suggesting that laboratory biomarkers carried substantially more predictive information than symptom indicators alone.

Feature importance analysis further supported these findings. Variables such as prothrombin, platelets, albumin, bilirubin, and age were identified as some of the most influential predictors. These features are clinically meaningful because they are closely associated with liver function, coagulation behavior, and disease severity.

Random Forest Feature Importance

Additional engineered features were also explored to improve interpretability while preserving classification performance. Features such as coagulation risk metrics, bilirubin-albumin ratios, Child-Pugh-inspired indicators, and logarithmic transformations of laboratory measurements were introduced into the model. The engineered-feature Random Forest achieved 88.35% testing accuracy, remaining very close to the baseline model while providing more interpretable clinical relationships.

Overall, the Random Forest experiments demonstrated three major findings. First, Random Forest models were highly effective for multiclass liver cirrhosis classification. Second, laboratory measurements represented the most important feature group within the dataset. Third, feature engineering improved interpretability while maintaining strong overall model performance.

MLP Neural Network Results

The custom multilayer perceptron (MLP) neural network achieved a validation accuracy of approximately 87.33% and a testing accuracy of 87.76% when classifying liver cirrhosis stages 1–3. The relatively small difference between validation and testing performance suggests that the network generalized reasonably well to unseen data without severe overfitting.

MLP Confusion Matrix

MLP Training Loss

The training loss curve demonstrated stable convergence throughout the training process, with loss steadily decreasing across the 35,000 training epochs. Confusion matrix analysis also showed strong overall classification performance, with most predictions concentrated along the diagonal of the matrix. Similar to the other models explored in this project, the majority of misclassifications occurred between neighboring disease stages due to overlapping clinical indicators between adjacent stages of cirrhosis progression.

Although the MLP achieved strong classification performance, the improvement relative to the Random Forest model remained modest. This suggests that limitations in dataset size, feature separability, and overlap between clinical stages may have constrained the maximum achievable classification accuracy more than the specific model architecture itself.

CNN Results

The convolutional neural network (CNN) achieved a highest validation accuracy of approximately 82% and a testing accuracy near 80% when classifying liver fibrosis stages f0–f4. Although the CNN did not outperform the strongest tabular machine learning models explored earlier in the project, the results demonstrated that meaningful fibrosis-related imaging patterns could still be identified directly from ultrasound data.

CNN Confusion Matrix

CNN Training Performance

Earlier CNN configurations achieved testing and validation accuracies closer to 70–75%. As the architecture and training configuration were refined, increasing the number of training images improved overall classification performance by exposing the network to a broader range of fibrosis-related imaging features. Initial development iterations used smaller image subsets in order to reduce processing time while evaluating architectural changes and training parameters before expanding to larger balanced datasets.

Training performance was also influenced by hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, validation frequency, and maximum training epochs. Validation monitoring and early stopping techniques were implemented to terminate training once validation performance stopped improving, reducing unnecessary computation and helping preserve the best-performing validation network.

The confusion matrix showed that most classifications were concentrated along the diagonal, indicating generally successful prediction across the fibrosis categories. However, misclassifications still occurred between neighboring fibrosis stages, suggesting that additional improvements in feature extraction and dataset diversity may further improve CNN classification performance in future work.

Conclusion and Future Work

This project explored multiple machine learning approaches for liver cirrhosis stage classification using both structured clinical datasets and medical imaging data. Decision Tree, Random Forest, Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and preliminary CNN-based approaches were evaluated using publicly available liver cirrhosis datasets in MATLAB.

Among the tabular models, Random Forest achieved the highest overall performance with testing accuracies approaching 89%, while the custom MLP neural network achieved comparable performance near 88%. The relatively small performance gap between the Random Forest and MLP models suggests that limitations in dataset size, feature separability, and the complexity of nonlinear relationships within the structured clinical data may have reduced the advantages typically associated with deeper neural network architectures.

Feature analysis showed that biomarkers such as bilirubin, albumin, platelets, and prothrombin were among the most important predictors of cirrhosis severity.

The CNN-based image classification pipeline achieved encouraging preliminary results and demonstrated the broader potential of combining structured clinical records with medical imaging analysis for liver disease assessment.

Future work may include larger and more diverse datasets, improved neural network optimization techniques, deeper CNN architectures, transfer learning approaches, and multimodal systems capable of simultaneously analyzing clinical records and medical imaging data.
